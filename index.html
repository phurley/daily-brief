Science & technology

Room-temperature device produces correlated microwave pairs

MIT researchers demonstrated a compact magnon–photon device that splits one input microwave photon into two frequency-distinct, strongly correlated signals at room temperature, then used them to recover an encoded image in a secure-communications demonstration.

What’s new: Published in Nature Electronics on August 19, the peer-reviewed result replaces a cryogenic superconducting approach with a room-temperature cavity-magnonic platform and demonstrates non-degenerate correlated outputs. Caveat: It remains a laboratory proof of principle, the authors disclose a related patent filing, and a preprint preceded this version of record. Paper · MIT summary

Hubble data point to an earlier major Milky Way merger

A Nature Astronomy analysis of homogeneous Hubble photometry for 39 inner-Milky-Way globular clusters identifies a third age–metallicity sequence, attributed to a merger with the proposed Low-energy-Kraken-Heracles progenitor about 12.3 billion years ago.

What’s new: Published August 17, the peer-reviewed study dates the event about 1.8 billion years before the Gaia-Sausage-Enceladus merger. Caveat: The merger is inferred from cluster ages, chemical-evolution modeling, and orbital statistics rather than directly observed; absolute ages depend on stellar-evolution models, and smaller progenitors without surviving clusters cannot be ruled out. Paper · NASA summary

LRO resolves a fresh lunar crater from a Falcon 9 upper-stage impact

NASA’s Lunar Reconnaissance Orbiter imaged the crater created when a Falcon 9 upper stage struck the Moon on August 5. Multi-angle camera images put it at about 60 feet wide and under 10 feet deep, with older weathered material and fresher ejecta exposed.

What’s new: NASA released the measurements August 18; the observation also tested impact-prediction tools after an independently refined trajectory and Korea’s Danuri imaging helped localize the site. Caveat: This is an official mission observation, not a peer-reviewed paper. Depth comes from shadow length, and one accidental impact cannot establish general impact behavior. NASA mission report
